On February 2, 2026, the FDA made a big change to how medical device companies manage quality. It replaced the old Quality System Regulation (QSR) with the new Quality Management System Regulation (QMSR).

Because the new rule uses ISO 13485:2016 as its base, some teams expected US labeling rules to become simpler. If you already follow ISO 13485, it is easy to think your labeling process is all set.

It is not quite that simple. The FDA felt ISO 13485 did not go into enough detail on labeling and packaging, so it kept its own extra rules in place.

Here is what changed, what stayed strict, and what labeling teams need to watch for.

## What QMSR actually changed

Since 1997, medical device makers in the US have followed the Quality System Regulation (QSR) in 21 CFR Part 820. Companies selling in the US and in other countries often had to keep up with two different quality systems: the QSR and ISO 13485.

QMSR brings the US closer to the rest of the world by building ISO 13485:2016 right into 21 CFR Part 820.

Still, ISO 13485 does not replace US law. Where the FDA wanted tighter controls, it added extra sections on top of the ISO standard. One of the two main extra sections is about device labeling and packaging.

## The labeling rule FDA kept: 21 CFR 820.45

Under QMSR, labeling and packaging are covered by **21 CFR 820.45**. It adds to ISO 13485 Clause 7.5.1.

The rule says you must have written procedures to keep labels and packaging intact, inspected, properly stored, and correctly applied through storage, handling, distribution, and use. In day-to-day work, it comes down to three things.

### 1. Check five details before release or storage

Before labeling or packaging is released or put into storage, someone has to check it for accuracy. Under 21 CFR 820.45(a), that check must include these details, where they apply:

- The right Unique Device Identifier (UDI), UPC, or other device ID
- The expiration date
- Storage instructions
- Handling instructions
- Any extra processing instructions

If even one of these values changes in a spec sheet and does not make it onto the label, your label is wrong.

### 2. Record the label release

Under 21 CFR 820.45(b), you have to document the release of labeling for use, following ISO 13485 Clause 4.2.5 (Control of records).

A quick "looks good" buried in an email thread is hard to find, hard to control, and easy to lose. A good release record clearly shows which version of the label was released, who released it, and when.

### 3. Prevent mix-ups before use

Under 21 CFR 820.45(c), you need set processes that prevent mix-ups. Your team must inspect labels and packaging before use to make sure every device gets the right label and package, matching what is in the medical device file (the file that describes your device and its labeling). You also have to keep a written record of that check.

## UDI now connects to more of your records

The other main extra rule in QMSR is **21 CFR 820.35** (Control of records).

Under this rule, the UDI appears in more than your labels. Manufacturers must record the device's UDI (or UPC, if it has one) in complaint records and servicing records, and must record the UDI for each device or batch they make.

This means your labeling data and your quality records have to match. If the UDI printed on a label does not line up with your product file, your GUDID entry (the FDA's public UDI database, required separately under 21 CFR Part 830), or your complaint logs, that gap can come up during an inspection.

## How FDA inspections changed

Along with QMSR, the FDA stopped using its old inspection method (QSIT) on February 2, 2026. Investigators now follow an updated compliance program, **Compliance Program 7382.850**.

Two changes matter a lot for labeling and quality teams:

- **Audit and review records are no longer off-limits.** Under the old QSR, FDA inspectors generally could not ask to see internal audit reports, supplier audit reports, or management review records. Under QMSR, they can. If your internal audit missed a labeling issue, or flagged one that was never fixed, the inspector can see it.
- **Risk management is getting close attention.** Early reviews of 2026 Form 483s (the list of issues an investigator gives you at the end of an inspection), including analyses from [Redica Systems](https://www.redica.com/resources/post/six-months-of-fda-device-gmp-enforcement-under-the-qmsr) and the [FDA Law Blog](https://www.thefdalawblog.com/2026/08/the-qmsr-is-here-fdas-first-qmsr-warning-letters/), show that ISO 13485 Clause 7.1, which covers planning and risk management, is among the most cited clauses. Because investigators use your risk files as a starting point, it helps when changes, including label changes, clearly connect back to your risk records.

## A simple checklist for labeling teams

If your team sells medical devices in the US, here are five practical things to check now:

1. **Update your SOP references.** Make sure your labeling procedures no longer point to the old QSR sections (like 820.120 and 820.130) and instead match 21 CFR 820.45 and ISO 13485.
2. **Build the five required checks into every review.** Make sure reviewers check UDI, expiration date, storage instructions, handling instructions, and processing instructions (where they apply) before release.
3. **Keep clean release records.** Make it easy to find the released version, when it was released, and who released it.
4. **Match UDI data everywhere.** Confirm that the UDI on your label matches your medical device file, your GUDID entry, and your complaint and servicing forms.
5. **Audit your labeling process properly.** Since inspectors can now read your internal audits, make sure your internal checks actually test labeling accuracy and version control.
